Module 6, Part II: TEC Process Model And The Simulation¶

This is the second part of Module 6. Complete Part I: P/PI Control And Lumped Modeling first. Both parts use the same prepared simulation: Part I emphasizes the one-lump model, while Part II compares the one- and two-lump models.

Introductory Material¶

Purpose¶

This part follows the first Module 6 modeling work and the initial physical PI implementation. Before beginning Part II, complete the physical \(K_p\) and \(K_i\) study and generate the eight experimental plots required by Module 6, Part I, A3 Question 2. Part II connects three things:

  1. the physical TEC/block/thermistor system,
  2. the feedback-control equations,
  3. a Python simulation GUI that lets you change model parameters and watch the predicted temperature response.

This module is not about perfect quantitative prediction. It is about learning how a simple model can qualitatively and quantitatively explain droop, overshoot, lag, damping, and the transition from stable to unstable control.

Throughout Part II, use two parallel mathematical descriptions of the same dynamics. The coupled first-order energy balances form a state-space matrix whose eigenvalues determine the exponential modes. Eliminating the hidden states produces one higher-order differential equation whose characteristic polynomial has the same roots. Moving between these descriptions is a central reason for studying the two-lump model.

Learning Objectives¶

By the end of Module 6, Part II, you should be able to:

  • write and interpret the coupled energy balances for the TEC-side and measured-block thermal masses;
  • show how two coupled first-order temperature equations produce one second-order equation for the measured temperature;
  • use the two-lump model to explain how thermal storage and transport produce phase lag, overshoot, and behavior absent from the one-lump P model;
  • calculate \(\zeta_{\mathrm{2L,P}}\) and \(K_{p,\mathrm{crit}}\) and use them to classify two-lump P responses as over-, critically, or underdamped;
  • calculate \(K_{i,\mathrm{crit}}\) and \(\rho_I=K_i/K_{i,\mathrm{crit}}\) and use the Routh-Hurwitz criterion to predict stable, marginal, or unstable two-lump PI behavior;
  • predict and verify how \(K_p\), \(K_i\), \(G\), total capacitance, and capacitance division affect response speed, damping, and stability;
  • hold all physical model parameters fixed while varying only \(K_p\) and \(K_i\), so changes caused by controller tuning can be distinguished from changes to the modeled apparatus;
  • characterize matched physical P and PI responses using rise time, overshoot, settling time, steady-state error, and oscillation period;
  • generate eight simulation Goldilocks plots using one shared set of physical parameters and compare them with the eight experimental Goldilocks plots;
  • test whether one- and two-lump P models can reproduce small, critical, underdamped, and sustained-oscillation behavior, and use a failure to reproduce sustained oscillation as evidence that a model is incomplete; and
  • select and justify \(K_p\) and \(K_i\) by balancing rapid approach to the setpoint against overshoot, settling time, actuator limits, and stability while connecting theory, simulation, and measurement.

The eight measured and eight simulated Goldilocks plots are the principal way you achieve these objectives. They are assessed together with the two-lump derivation in A3 Questions 3 and 5.

The parallel matrix/eigenvalue and higher-order differential-equation routes are included for mathematical culture and interpretation. Follow how they produce the same characteristic polynomial, but do not prepare a separate A3 derivation of the matrix formalism.

Class Theme¶

Use Models To Explain Measurements

In control theory, the process is the physical cause-and-effect system that turns the controller output into a measured response. Here, the PWM command changes the heat transported by the TEC; thermal capacitances store energy; thermal conductances move heat through the aluminum block and to the room; and the thermistor measures the resulting temperature. This chain from PWM command to measured temperature is the process we are modeling.

Vocabulary¶

  • Process: the physical system being controlled. Here, the process is the TEC/block/thermistor thermal system.
  • State variable: a number that describes the current state of the model, such as temperature.
  • Heat: energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Heat is not the same thing as temperature; temperature tells how hot something is, while heat is energy moving into or out of the system.
  • Lumped model: a model that treats an extended object as if the entire object were at the same temperature. The lump can be viewed as a point object of finite thermal mass. See the one-lump/two-lump figure.
  • Thermal lag: delay between changing the actuator and observing the measured temperature response.
  • Saturation: the actuator command reaches its limit, such as PWM 255. This could be a physical or software imposed limit.
  • Stable: the response returns to steady state after a disturbance.
  • Underdamped: a stable response with oscillations that decrease in amplitude.
  • Marginally stable: an idealized response with constant-amplitude oscillations that continue forever.
  • Unstable: a response that moves farther from steady state; oscillations grow rather than decay when the instability is oscillatory.

Model Equations And Numerical Algorithm¶

The Python GUI separates the physical model from the controller. Select either a one-lump or two-lump process, then select open-loop, P, or PI control.

Course-specific lumped-model diagram.

The one-lump model assigns one temperature to the TEC, block, and thermistor assembly. It retains heat storage and heat loss to the room, but it cannot represent an internal temperature difference or delay. The two-lump model separates the TEC-side temperature \(T\) from the measured temperature \(T_m\). Heat first crosses the internal conductance \(G\) and then leaves the measured lump through the room conductance \(H\). Because the controller responds to \(T_m\), this extra thermal store and transport step introduce lag that can produce overshoot and underdamped behavior.

One-Lump Physical Model¶

Part I developed the one-lump energy balance and its measured-parameter form. Carry those results forward rather than deriving them again. For comparison with the two-lump model, the one-lump capacitance is the sum

\[ C=C_T+C_m, \]

and the simulation continues to use \(P_{u,h}\) for heating and \(P_{u,c}\) for cooling, with

\[ r=\frac{P_{u,h}}{P_{u,c}}=\frac{\chi_h}{\chi_c}. \]

Two-Lump Physical Model¶

The two-lump model separates the TEC-side temperature \(T\) from the measured temperature \(T_m\):

\[ C_T\frac{dT}{dt}=P_u(u)u-G(T-T_m), \]
\[ C_m\frac{dT_m}{dt}=G(T-T_m)-H(T_m-T_{\mathrm{amb}}). \]

Here \(C_T\) and \(C_m\) are thermal capacitances. The conductance \(G\) couples the two lumps through the passive heat flow \(G(T-T_m)\). The same heat that leaves the TEC-side lump enters the measured lump, so this term appears with opposite signs in the two equations. We simplify the apparatus by giving the TEC-side lump no separate path to the room; all heat loss to the room is represented by \(H(T_m-T_{\mathrm{amb}})\) in the measured-lump equation.

The controller responds to \(T_m\), but \(T\) and \(T_m\) do not change at the same time. Which temperature changes first if someone opens a window and \(T_{\mathrm{amb}}\) suddenly decreases? Which changes first if \(T_{\mathrm{set}}\) changes? This lag can produce overshoot or oscillation.

Two routes through the two-lump model

We will preserve both descriptions used in Part I.

  • Matrix route: retain the TEC-side and measured-block temperatures as separate state variables. The eigenvalues of their coupled first-order matrix give the exponential time factors.
  • Higher-order ODE route: eliminate the hidden TEC-side temperature. The measured temperature then obeys one second-order equation under P control. Its characteristic roots are the same eigenvalues.

The matrix emphasizes interacting physical states. The higher-order equation emphasizes the measured output and makes damping formulas easier to recognize.

From Two First-Order Equations To One Second-Order Equation¶

Consider one heating or cooling direction so that \(P_u\) is constant, assume the PWM is not saturated, and use P control:

\[ u=K_p(T_{\mathrm{set}}-T_m). \]

Let \(\theta_T\) and \(\theta_m\) be deviations of \(T\) and \(T_m\) from their steady-state values. The constant steady-state terms cancel, leaving

\[ C_T\dot\theta_T=-G\theta_T+(G-P_uK_p)\theta_m, \]
\[ C_m\dot\theta_m=G\theta_T-(G+H)\theta_m. \]
Matrix Route: Two-Lump P Eigenvalues

Keep both temperature displacements in the state vector,

\[ \mathbf{x}= \begin{pmatrix}\theta_T\\\theta_m\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{x}}=A_P\mathbf{x}, \]

with

\[ A_P= \begin{pmatrix} -\dfrac{G}{C_T} & \dfrac{G-P_uK_p}{C_T}\\[6pt] \dfrac{G}{C_m} & -\dfrac{G+H}{C_m} \end{pmatrix}. \]

An exponential state mode has the form \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}e^{st}\). A nonzero eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}\) exists only when

\[ \det(sI-A_P)=0. \]

Evaluating the determinant gives

\[ \boxed{ C_TC_m s^2+ \left[C_T(G+H)+C_mG\right]s+ G(H+P_uK_p)=0 }. \]

This is precisely the characteristic polynomial obtained below by eliminating \(\theta_T\). Real negative eigenvalues give sums of decaying exponentials. A complex-conjugate pair gives a real decaying oscillation: the real part sets the decay rate and the imaginary part sets the oscillation frequency.

The second equation gives the hidden TEC-side temperature in terms of the measured temperature and its rate of change:

\[ G\theta_T=C_m\dot\theta_m+(G+H)\theta_m. \]

Differentiate the second equation, use the first equation to replace \(\dot\theta_T\), and then use the expression above to replace \(\theta_T\). The result is one second-order differential equation for the measured temperature:

\[ \boxed{ C_TC_m\ddot\theta_m+ \left[C_T(G+H)+C_mG\right]\dot\theta_m+ G(H+P_uK_p)\theta_m=0. } \]

Thus two coupled first-order equations for two stored-energy states are equivalent to one second-order equation for \(T_m\). Substituting \(\theta_m\propto e^{st}\) gives

\[ C_TC_m s^2+ \left[C_T(G+H)+C_mG\right]s+ G(H+P_uK_p)=0. \]

Divide by \(C_TC_m\) and compare with the standard second-order form

\[ s^2+2\zeta\omega_n s+\omega_n^2=0. \]

The two-lump P-control natural frequency and damping ratio are

\[ \boxed{ \omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{G(H+P_uK_p)}{C_TC_m}} }, \]
\[ \boxed{ \zeta_{\mathrm{2L,P}} =\frac{C_T(G+H)+C_mG} {2\sqrt{C_TC_mG(H+P_uK_p)}} }. \]

This expression predicts the transition:

  • \(\zeta_{\mathrm{2L,P}}>1\) gives an overdamped response;
  • \(\zeta_{\mathrm{2L,P}}=1\) gives critical damping; and
  • \(\zeta_{\mathrm{2L,P}}<1\) gives an underdamped response with overshoot or ringing.

Because all coefficients in this second-order P-control equation are positive, the two-lump P model is stable for positive physical parameters and negative feedback. Its underdamped oscillations decay. Sustained oscillations that last forever or oscillations that grow are not predictions of this linear two-lump P model; observing either would falsify at least one of its assumptions or reveal omitted effects such as delay, saturation, or additional thermal states.

For fixed physical parameters, increasing \(K_p\) increases the denominator and therefore decreases \(\zeta_{\mathrm{2L,P}}\). Sufficiently large P gain can move the two-lump model from overdamped to underdamped behavior. The critical gain is found by setting \(\zeta_{\mathrm{2L,P}}=1\):

\[ \boxed{ K_{p,\mathrm{crit}} =\frac{1}{P_u} \left[ \frac{\left(C_T(G+H)+C_mG\right)^2} {4C_TC_mG}-H \right] }. \]

The dependence on \(G\) is not monotonic. Very large \(G\) forces \(T\approx T_m\) and recovers one-lump behavior. Very small \(G\) nearly disconnects the measured mass from the TEC, producing a very slow response. For fixed \(C_T\), \(C_m\), and \(H\), the damping ratio is smallest at

\[ G=\frac{C_TH}{C_T+C_m}. \]

Likewise, at fixed total capacitance \(C=C_T+C_m\), putting almost all the capacitance in either lump suppresses the second-order behavior. The damping ratio is smallest when

\[ \frac{C_T}{C}=\frac{G}{H+2G}, \]

which is close to one-half when \(G\gg H\). Thus a noticeable internal lag and two appreciable thermal stores promote ringing; neither phrase alone means that making \(G\) arbitrarily small always creates more oscillation.

Complex eigenvalues produce an underdamped transient even though the apparatus contains no mechanical momentum. Thermal storage in two locations and delayed transport between them supply the two dynamical states.

Stability Of Two-Lump PI Control¶

PI control adds the integral state

\[ q=\int e\,dt,\qquad \dot q=-\theta_m, \]

and the command perturbation is

\[ u=-K_p\theta_m+K_iq. \]
Two-Lump PI: Three First-Order Equations Or One Third-Order Equation

Let \(z=q-q_{\mathrm{ss}}\) and retain the three states \(\mathbf{x}=(\theta_T,\theta_m,z)^T\). Their matrix equation is

\[ \dot{\mathbf{x}}=A_{PI}\mathbf{x}, \qquad A_{PI}= \begin{pmatrix} -\dfrac{G}{C_T} & \dfrac{G-P_uK_p}{C_T} & \dfrac{P_uK_i}{C_T}\\[6pt] \dfrac{G}{C_m} & -\dfrac{G+H}{C_m} & 0\\[6pt] 0 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The matrix route uses \(\det(sI-A_{PI})=0\) to find three eigenvalues and three exponential factors \(e^{s_jt}\). Alternatively, eliminate \(\theta_T\) and \(z\). The measured-temperature displacement then obeys

\[ \boxed{ C_TC_m\frac{d^3\theta_m}{dt^3} +\left[C_T(G+H)+C_mG\right]\frac{d^2\theta_m}{dt^2} +G(H+P_uK_p)\frac{d\theta_m}{dt} +GP_uK_i\theta_m=0 }. \]

Substituting \(\theta_m\propto e^{st}\) gives the same cubic as the matrix determinant. The two descriptions therefore agree mode by mode. A real eigenvalue contributes a purely growing or decaying exponential; a complex-conjugate pair contributes an oscillation whose envelope grows or decays according to the sign of the pair's real part.

Substitution into the two-lump equations gives the characteristic equation

\[ \boxed{ C_TC_m s^3+ \left[C_T(G+H)+C_mG\right]s^2+ G(H+P_uK_p)s+ GP_uK_i=0. } \]

This equation is third order, so the complete response does not have one second-order damping ratio \(\zeta\). It commonly has one real eigenvalue and a complex-conjugate pair; that pair produces the observed ringing.

Write the cubic as \(a_3s^3+a_2s^2+a_1s+a_0=0\). All four coefficients above are positive. The Routh-Hurwitz stability condition therefore reduces to

\[ a_2a_1>a_3a_0. \]

The significance of the Routh-Hurwitz criterion is that it determines stability from the polynomial coefficients without explicitly calculating all three eigenvalues. For the convention used here, each free mode varies as \(e^{st}\), so asymptotic stability requires every eigenvalue \(s\) to have a negative real part, not to be positive. The Routh-Hurwitz conditions are necessary and sufficient for that result.

Solving the equality for the integral gain gives the stability boundary

\[ \boxed{ K_{i,\mathrm{crit}} =\frac{\left[C_T(G+H)+C_mG\right](H+P_uK_p)} {C_TC_mP_u}. } \]

A useful dimensionless measure of integral aggressiveness is

\[ \boxed{\rho_I=\frac{K_i}{K_{i,\mathrm{crit}}}.} \]

As \(\rho_I\) approaches 1 from below, the oscillatory pair becomes less damped. At \(\rho_I=1\), the normalized polynomial factors as \((s+a)(s^2+b)\): one eigenvalue remains negative while the other two are purely imaginary. The linear model then predicts sustained oscillation. For \(\rho_I>1\), it predicts growing oscillation and instability.

The qualitative classification is therefore:

  • \(0<\rho_I<1\): stable; any oscillations decay;
  • \(\rho_I=1\): marginally stable; ideal constant-amplitude oscillations persist forever; and
  • \(\rho_I>1\): unstable; oscillations grow.

This distinction is useful for model falsification. A stable underdamped trace may overshoot many times, but its envelope must shrink. A trace with constant amplitude lies on the predicted stability boundary, while a growing envelope lies beyond it. If the measured classification disagrees with the model's classification for the measured parameters, the model or one of its assumed parameter values is inadequate.

The prediction of exponential growth applies only while the controller and plant remain linear. The apparatus cannot apply an arbitrarily large command: the H-bridge limits the signed command to \(-255\leq u\leq+255\) PWM. Once the requested command reaches either limit, the applied command is clipped and the linear characteristic equation no longer describes the complete response. Instead of growing forever, the simulated or physical temperature may approach a bounded nonlinear oscillation. This does not make the underlying linear model stable; it shows that actuator saturation has limited the instability.

These results give practical rules of thumb:

Change Expected effect Interpretation
Increase \(K_i\) Increases \(\rho_I\) and promotes oscillation Integral action removes droop, but too much increases settling time or causes instability.
Increase \(K_p\) at fixed \(K_i\) Raises \(K_{i,\mathrm{crit}}\) P action can improve the PI stability margin, although excessive \(K_p\) can already excite two-lump P overshoot.
Decrease \(G\) over the apparatus-relevant range Increases internal lag and usually reduces PI stability margin The integral continues accumulating while \(T_m\) responds slowly. Extremely small or large \(G\) requires direct evaluation rather than this slogan.
Make both \(C_T\) and \(C_m\) appreciable Strengthens two-state behavior Concentrating nearly all capacitance in one lump moves the system toward one-lump behavior.
Increase total \(C\) without retuning gains Slows the plant and generally makes the same \(K_i\) more aggressive More error can accumulate before the measured temperature responds.
Increase \(P_u\) Makes each PWM unit more effective The same numerical \(K_p\) and \(K_i\) produce stronger physical feedback.
Saturate PWM with anti-windup off Produces integral windup The resulting overshoot is nonlinear controller memory, not solely the linear two-lump instability.

The objective is not the shortest rise time at any cost. A large gain may reach the setpoint quickly on the first crossing but then overshoot and take longer to settle. A useful tuning procedure is:

  1. choose \(K_p\) for a fast P response without unacceptable overshoot;
  2. choose a slightly smaller \(K_p\) when adding integral action;
  3. increase \(K_i\) from zero until droop is removed on the desired timescale;
  4. stop increasing \(K_i\) when extra oscillation makes the settling time worse;
  5. verify both heating and cooling steps without sustained saturation.

At steady state, both temperature derivatives are zero, so

\[ P_u u=G(T-T_m)=H(T_m-T_{\mathrm{amb}}). \]

The internal conductance \(G\) sets the temperature difference between the two lumps, but it cancels from the overall balance \(P_u u=H(T_m-T_{\mathrm{amb}})\). This is the quantitative reason the one-lump model can predict the final measured temperature and P-control droop even when it fails to predict the high-gain transient.

Controller Choices¶

The measured temperature is \(T\) for the one-lump model and \(T_m\) for the two-lump model. Define

\[ e=T_{\mathrm{set}}-T_{\mathrm{meas}}. \]

The three controller choices are

\[ u=u_{\mathrm{user}}\quad\text{(open loop)}, \]
\[ u=K_pe\quad\text{(P control)}, \]
\[ u=K_pe+K_i\int e\,dt=u_P+u_I\quad\text{(PI control)}. \]

The signed command is clamped to the PWM limit. If the anti-windup option is enabled, the program prevents the integral state from continuing to grow when the actuator is saturated in the same direction as the error.

What The Algorithm Does At Each Time Step¶

The simulation described in How To Run The Python GUI performs the numerical work, but you should understand its sequence:

  1. Read the model's measured temperature, \(T_n\) or \(T_{m,n}\).
  2. Calculate the error \(e_n\).
  3. For PI control, propose the integral update \(q_{n+1}=q_n+e_n\Delta t\). Accept it unless anti-windup prevents the update; otherwise use \(q_{n+1}=q_n\).
  4. Calculate the requested command for the selected controller: \(u_n=u_{\mathrm{user}}\) for open-loop control, \(u_n=K_pe_n\) for P control, or \(u_n=K_pe_n+K_iq_{n+1}\) for PI control.
  5. Clamp \(u_n\) to the signed PWM limit.
  6. Select \(P_{u,h}\) or \(P_{u,c}\) from the sign of the applied command.
  7. Evaluate the heat-balance derivatives and advance \(T\) and, for the two-lump model, \(T_m\) by one Euler step.

For example, the first two-lump Euler updates are

\[ T_{n+1}=T_n+\frac{\Delta t}{C_T} \left[P_u(u_n)u_n-G(T_n-T_{m,n})\right], \]
\[ T_{m,n+1}=T_{m,n}+\frac{\Delta t}{C_m} \left[G(T_n-T_{m,n})-H(T_{m,n}-T_{\mathrm{amb}})\right]. \]

You are not required to write this code. Use it to develop physical intuition: vary one model, parameter, or initial condition at a time; predict the result; observe the response; and interpret it using the heat balances and controller equations.

Two-Lump Theory Assignment¶

  2. Identify which term describes heat loss from the measured block to room air.

  4. Explain why a controller that uses Tm may react too late.
  5. Divide the measured-lump equation by \(C_m\) and compare it term by term with the rate-constant form \(dT_m/dt=k_{21}(T-T_m)-k_m(T_m-T_{\mathrm{amb}})\). Looking Ahead: From Lumps To A Temperature Field

The two-lump model is a crude spatial approximation. It replaces an extended apparatus with two internally uniform regions connected by a thermal conductance. It distinguishes a region near the TEC from a region near the thermistor, but it contains no continuous distance, temperature gradient, or spatial coordinate.

The long-rod experiment cannot be described by a single temperature. It needs a temperature field that depends on longitudinal position and time:

\[ T = T(x,t) \]

For the rod we will move toward equations of the form

\[ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2} \]

with an additional term that represents heat loss from the sides to the room:

\[ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2} - \beta\left(T - T_{\mathrm{room}}\right) \]

In these equations, alpha is the thermal diffusivity in m²/s and beta is a side-loss rate constant in 1/s.

You do not need to solve these equations or submit written answers about them in Module 6. They are included to show where the lumped-model sequence leads.
