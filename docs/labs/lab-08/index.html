Module 7, Part III: Rod Instrument, Step Response, And Stationary Fin¶

This is the experimental part of Module 7. It builds on Part I: Thermal Transport Foundations and Part II: Finite And Infinite Rod Theory.

Purpose¶

In Modules 1-6, one or two temperatures were enough to describe the TEC process. The aluminum rod cannot be treated as one thermal lump. Its temperature depends on position and time:

\[ T=T(x,t). \]

In this module you will build and validate the multichannel rod instrument, measure a non-oscillatory step response, and measure the steady temperature profile. The steady profile gives the first quantitative evidence of heat loss from the rod's side. The sinusoidal Angstrom experiment begins in Module 8.

Thermistor Channels And Calibration¶

The Arduino Uno R3 has six analog input channels, A0 through A5, feeding one multiplexed 10-bit analog-to-digital converter. Use one channel for the thermistor in the TEC base and the other five channels for five thermistors along the rod. The converter samples the six channels in rapid sequence rather than at exactly the same instant. This delay is negligible on the thermal timescales measured here.

The purpose here is to assign each thermistor a permanent channel, cross-calibrate all six measurement chains, and establish the sensor map used for the non-oscillatory step response and stationary-fin profile. Module 8 reuses the same calibrated sensor map for the oscillatory Angstrom experiment. The rod has more thermistor holes than the five available rod channels, so the five rod thermistors can be distributed among selected holes to obtain a useful range of positions and temperatures. Holes in the first group are nominally spaced at \(1/2\)-inch intervals; holes in the second group are nominally spaced at \(1\)-inch intervals. Treat these as orientation values only; measure and record the actual positions you use.

The course rod is 6061-T6 aluminum with a nominal diameter of \(1/2\) inch, or \(12.7\ \mathrm{mm}\). The analysis base at \(x=0\) is the first thermistor hole in the rod, occupied by rod thermistor 1. It is not the physical end of the rod and it is not the thermistor embedded in the TEC. Measure the positions of the other four rod thermistors relative to this origin. Define the analyzed length \(L\) as the distance from the first thermistor hole to the rod tip. Also record the rod's overall physical length, diameter \(d\), and their uncertainties. The TEC thermistor monitors the actuator, while the measured temperature of rod thermistor 1 supplies the boundary condition \(T(0,t)\) for the rod model.

Use one model-testing cycle throughout Part III:

  1. Predict: use the Part I and Part II theory to predict the qualitative transient, the stationary profile, and the importance of the end and radius.
  2. Measure: acquire synchronized, calibrated temperature data with the geometry and settings needed to interpret them.
  3. Compare: fit the finite and semi-infinite models and inspect residuals, parameter values, and uncertainties.
  4. Judge: state which assumptions the evidence supports and where the model is inadequate.

Learning Objectives¶

The objectives below are listed in priority order. By the end of Module 7, Part III, you should be able to:

  1. Build and document a spatial temperature measurement by defining sensor coordinates, cross-checking the thermistors, and acquiring synchronized \(T(x,t)\) data with units, timestamps, and experimental metadata.
  2. Use the finite-length solution and transverse Biot number to make separate, quantitative decisions about whether the far end and radial temperature variation may be neglected.
  3. Distinguish a transient from a steady spatial profile and explain why a temperature disturbance arrives later and is smaller farther from the heated base.
  4. Fit finite-length and semi-infinite stationary-fin models, examine their residuals, and interpret the fitted decay coefficient and decay length.
  5. Explain what the steady experiment can determine and why it cannot determine the thermal conductivity and side heat-transfer coefficient separately.

Part III Vocabulary And Notation¶

Review the foundational vocabulary in Part I and the fin-model vocabulary in Part II. The terms below connect that theory to the experiment and its analysis.

Term Meaning
Temperature field, \(T(x,t)\) Temperature as a function of both position along the rod and time.
Environmental temperature, \(T_\infty\) The temperature of the environment far from the rod. In this experiment, it is the room temperature measured away from the heated apparatus.
Excess temperature, \(\theta(x,t)=T(x,t)-T_\infty\) Rod temperature measured relative to the environment.
Thermal diffusivity, \(\alpha=k/(\rho c)\) The coefficient controlling axial spreading in the Part III model; units m\(^2\)/s. This is Lienhard's notation.
Side-loss rate, \(\nu=hP/(\rho cA)\) The fractional rate, in s\(^{-1}\), at which side convection removes excess thermal energy in the one-dimensional model.
Decay coefficient, \(m=\sqrt{hP/(kA)}=\sqrt{\nu/\alpha}\) The spatial decay rate of the stationary profile; \(1/m\) is the decay length.
Sensor map A record of each channel name, thermistor position, coordinate origin and direction, and position uncertainty.
Calibration or cross-check A comparison under a common temperature condition used to identify sensor offsets or other channel differences.
Baseline Measurements made before the imposed temperature change, used to establish room temperature and initial sensor readings.
Step response The measured evolution of \(T(x,t)\) after the base-temperature command changes.
Boundary condition The measured or prescribed behavior at a boundary. For the rod model, the measured temperature of rod thermistor 1 at the first hole is the boundary condition \(T(0,t)\); the TEC setpoint and TEC-thermistor temperature describe the actuator that creates it.
Stationary profile The position-dependent temperature after changes with time have become negligible according to a stated criterion.
Model fit Estimation of model parameters by comparing a predicted curve with measured data.
Residual Measured value minus model prediction at the same position or time. Residual structure can reveal model inadequacy.
Uncertainty A quantitative estimate of the plausible range of a measured or fitted result.
Metadata Information needed to interpret and reproduce a run, including units, timestamps, channel labels, geometry, settings, and experimental notes.

Model¶

Part II set the energy-storage term to zero to obtain the stationary fin equation. We now restore that term while retaining distributed side cooling, so the model describes both the transient response and the stationary profile that it approaches.

Let

\[ \theta(x,t)=T(x,t)-T_\infty, \]

where \(T_\infty\) is the temperature of the environment far from the rod. For the course experiment, \(T_\infty\) is the room temperature measured away from the heated apparatus.

For a uniform rod with cross-sectional area \(A\), perimeter \(P\), density \(\rho\), specific heat \(c\), thermal conductivity \(k\), and side heat-transfer coefficient \(h\), conservation of energy gives

\[ \rho c A\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} =kA\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2}-hP\theta. \]

Every term has units of power per unit length, watts per meter. Dividing by \(\rho cA\) gives

\[ \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} =\alpha\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2}-\nu\theta, \qquad \alpha=\frac{k}{\rho c}, \qquad \nu=\frac{hP}{\rho c A}. \]

Here \(\alpha\) has units of square meters per second and \(\nu\) has units of inverse seconds.

At steady state,

\[ \frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2}-m^2\theta=0, \qquad m^2=\frac{hP}{kA}=\frac{\nu}{\alpha}. \]

This second-order equation requires two boundary conditions. For the analyzed section, rod thermistor 1 supplies the measured temperature at the analysis base \(x=0\), and the tip is treated as insulated. Part II then gives the complete finite-rod profile

\[ \frac{\theta(x)}{\theta(0)} =\frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)}. \]

Explore The Finite-Rod Model¶

Open the interactive TEC and finite-rod simulator, developed by Tim Hebert, and use a constant setpoint. The simulator numerically evolves the one-dimensional transient conduction equation with side heat loss, a finite rod, and a dynamic TEC boundary. Move the probe along the rod and observe both the approach to steady state and the final spatial profile. Use it to predict which parts of the measured rod should be sensitive to the far-end boundary condition before fitting the experimental data.

Suggested high-gain exploration. Place the probe at \(x=0\), use proportional control only, and compare successively larger values of \(K_p\), for example 8, 80, and 800. At modest gain, an overshoot can decay. At large gain, the simulator can instead settle into a sustained oscillation while the PWM repeatedly saturates or reverses. This oscillation is produced by the dynamic TEC actuator included in the simulator; it is absent from the ideal one-lump P-control model. Identify approximately where the qualitative change occurs, and compare the oscillation period with the thermal response times visible in the TEC and rod. Then hold \(K_p=800\) fixed and move the probe from \(x=0\) to a few positions farther along the rod, resetting between trials. Observe qualitatively that the oscillation frequency generally increases as the probe is moved closer to the TEC.

What physical model is inside this simulator?

The rod is divided into finite-difference elements that obey the one-dimensional transient heat equation

\[ \rho cA\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} =kA\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}-hP(T-T_\infty). \]

The simulator does not substitute the analytical cosh or exponential solution. It replaces the axial second derivative with centered finite differences on 24-100 rod elements and advances the resulting coupled ordinary differential equations with a 0.01 s numerical substep. The TEC balances and thermistor equation are advanced with the same substep, while the controller updates every 0.1 s. The analytical stationary profiles derived in Part II are therefore predictions that can be compared with the numerical long-time limit, not formulas built into the simulation.

The far tip has zero axial heat flux. The driven end is coupled to a dynamic TEC model. It is not an ideal heat source. The model has a cold-side temperature \(T_c\) and hot-side temperature \(T_h\), with separate heat capacities \(C_c\) and \(C_h\). A signed duty command \(u\) applies the averaged voltage \(V=uV_s\) to the TEC. The code calculates the signed current from this voltage, the TEC electrical resistance, and the Seebeck back-emf, then limits the current to the allowed range.

Using the conventional current \(I_c\) that is positive in the cooling direction, the heat absorbed at the cold side and rejected at the hot side are

\[ \dot Q_c =S I_c T_{c,K}-\frac{1}{2}I_c^2R-K_{\mathrm{TEC}}(T_h-T_c), \]
\[ \dot Q_h =S I_c T_{h,K}+\frac{1}{2}I_c^2R-K_{\mathrm{TEC}}(T_h-T_c). \]

Here \(S\) is the effective Seebeck coefficient, \(R\) is the electrical resistance, \(K_{\mathrm{TEC}}\) is the passive thermal conductance through the TEC, and the temperatures marked \(K\) are absolute temperatures. The terms proportional to \(SI_cT\) describe Peltier transport, the \(I_c^2R/2\) terms split Joule heating between the two faces, and the final terms describe ordinary conduction from the hot face toward the cold face.

The two TEC temperatures obey separate energy balances:

\[ C_c\frac{dT_c}{dt} =-\dot Q_c+G_c[T(0,t)-T_c]+G_{\mathrm{leak}}(T_\infty-T_c), \]
\[ C_h\frac{dT_h}{dt} =\dot Q_h-\frac{T_h-T_\infty}{R_{\mathrm{hs}}}. \]

The contact conductance \(G_c=h_{\mathrm{contact}}A\) couples the cold-side plate to the first finite-difference element of the rod. The hot side loses heat to the room through the heat-sink resistance \(R_{\mathrm{hs}}\), while \(G_{\mathrm{leak}}\) represents an additional cold-side leak to the room. The default parameters approximate a TEC1-12706-type device: \(S=0.0513\) V/K, \(R=1.861\ \Omega\), \(K_{\mathrm{TEC}}=0.560\) W/K, \(C_c=12\) J/K, \(C_h=180\) J/K, \(R_{\mathrm{hs}}=0.55\) K/W, and a 12 V supply. These values are model parameters, not measurements of the particular classroom TEC.

The rod is the distributed thermal load on this two-state actuator. Its first element exchanges heat with \(T_c\) through \(G_c\); the remaining elements conduct heat axially and lose heat from their sides. The movable thermistor measures the local rod temperature through a first-order sensor response with a 2 s time constant. The controller samples that measured temperature every 0.1 s and limits the signed PWM command to the range from \(-100\%\) to \(+100\%\).

High-gain P-control exploration. Put the probe at \(x=0\), turn off I and D, and keep all settings except \(K_p\) fixed. Reset the simulation for each trial. Begin with \(K_p=8\), then try \(K_p=80\) and \(K_p=800\), adding intermediate values to locate the transition. For each run, decide whether the response is monotonic, shows damped oscillations, or reaches a sustained oscillation. Record whether the PWM remains proportional or spends most of each cycle at \(+100\%\) and \(-100\%\), and compare the oscillation period with the visible TEC and rod response times.

Next set \(K_p=800\) and move the probe between \(x=0\) and a few downstream positions. Reset at each position and compare only the qualitative oscillation rate. The oscillations are generally faster near the TEC and slower when feedback is based on a more distant temperature.

For P control,

\[ u_{\mathrm{unsat}}=K_p[T_{\mathrm{set}}-T_s], \qquad u=\operatorname{sat}(u_{\mathrm{unsat}}). \]

As \(K_p\rightarrow\infty\), almost any nonzero error drives the command to one limit or the other. This is the bang-bang limit: full heating for one sign of the error and full cooling for the other. Reversing the command at the setpoint does not reverse the temperature immediately. Energy already stored in the TEC faces and rod keeps the temperature moving, so the system overshoots, reverses, and may form a bounded limit cycle.

The phase lag is cumulative. The separate \(C_c\) and \(C_h\) states give the TEC its own two-lump actuator lag. Finite contact conductance, the distributed diffusion modes of the rod, the thermistor response, and digital sampling add further lag. Placing the probe at \(x=0\) removes the additional propagation delay associated with measuring farther down the rod, but it does not remove the TEC dynamics or the rod's distributed thermal load. At sufficiently high loop gain, enough phase lag can make nominally negative feedback reinforce an oscillation at some frequency; PWM saturation then bounds its amplitude. This behavior is absent from the elementary one-lump P-control model, whose single closed-loop mode remains stable for positive parameters.

Moving the probe downstream adds thermal-diffusion delay and attenuates the measured oscillation. The controller therefore reacts to an older, weaker indication of what the actuator has done; in this simulator the sustained oscillations generally become slower as the probe moves away from the TEC. The exact distance scaling is not the point here because the TEC, rod, sensor, and saturation dynamics all contribute. The practical control-design lesson is more general: place the feedback sensor close to the actuator when the goal is fast, stable regulation, unless the temperature at a remote location is itself the quantity that must be controlled.

The model is intentionally idealized. It does not include every contact, temperature dependence, electronic delay, or three-dimensional heat-flow path in the physical apparatus.

When the far end has negligible influence over the measured region, this finite profile approaches the semi-infinite approximation

\[ \theta(x)=\theta_0 e^{-mx}. \]

The fitted \(m\) quantifies the competition between axial conduction and side loss. It does not determine \(k\) and \(h\) separately. The periodic Angstrom experiment in Module 8, Part I, supplies the second independent measurement.

Before The Experiment: Model-Validity Checks¶

Length: Solve It First¶

For the section of rod extending a distance \(L\) from the first thermistor hole to the insulated tip, the exact normalized stationary solution is

\[ \Theta_{\mathrm{finite}}(x) =\frac{\theta(x)}{\theta(0)} =\frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)}. \]

The semi-infinite model is the limiting expression

\[ \Theta_\infty(x)=e^{-mx}. \]

Calculate the relative error at every sensor position:

\[ \epsilon_T(x)= \frac{\left|\Theta_{\mathrm{finite}}(x)-\Theta_\infty(x)\right|} {\left|\Theta_{\mathrm{finite}}(x)\right|}. \]

State an acceptable error before deciding whether the far end may be ignored. A reasonable starting criterion is \(\epsilon_T<5\%\) at every sensor used in the analysis, but record and justify a different threshold if the intended measurement requires one. The product \(mL\) is useful, but the answer also depends on which sensor and which observable you use.

Radius: Check The Transverse Biot Number¶

For a circular rod,

\[ \mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}=\frac{h(A/P)}{k}=\frac{hR}{2k}. \]

The small-Biot condition \(\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}\ll1\) means radial conduction is fast enough that a cross section can be represented by one temperature. Before the experiment, estimate \(\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}\) using the rod radius and a justified range of \(h\) and \(k\). At this stage, proceed with the one-dimensional model under the provisional assumption that \(\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}\ll1\). You cannot yet test that assumption using the measured profile because \(m\) has not yet been measured.

After measuring the stationary temperature profile, return to this question. The fitted spatial decay coefficient obeys

\[ m^2=\frac{hP}{kA}, \]

so

\[ \frac{h}{k}=m^2\frac{A}{P}, \qquad \boxed{\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp} =m^2\left(\frac{A}{P}\right)^2}. \]

For a circular rod, \(A/P=R/2=d/4\), and therefore

\[ \boxed{\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}=\left(\frac{mR}{2}\right)^2 =\left(\frac{md}{4}\right)^2}. \]

Use the following measurement procedure:

  1. Calculate \(\theta_i=T_i-T_\infty\) from the stationary temperatures.
  2. Fit the finite-length profile first to obtain \(m\) and its uncertainty. This avoids assuming the semi-infinite limit before testing it.
  3. Use the measured diameter and the fitted \(m\) to calculate \(\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}\). Propagate the uncertainties in \(m\) and \(d\).
  4. Decide whether \(\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}\ll1\) adequately supports the assumption of a uniform temperature across each cross section.

This final calculation is a self-consistency check, not a logically independent proof: the one-dimensional profile used to fit \(m\) already assumes negligible radial temperature variation. If the fitted value gives \(\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}\ll1\), the assumption used at the beginning is self-consistent. If it does not, reject the one-dimensional interpretation of the fitted \(m\); a model with radial temperature variation would be required.

The same fitted \(m\) also completes the length check above. Calculate \(mL\) and evaluate \(\epsilon_T(x_i)\) at every sensor position. If the finite and semi-infinite profiles differ by less than your stated criterion throughout the measured region, the semi-infinite approximation is adequate there. If not, retain the finite-length solution. Thus \(\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}\) tests the radial approximation, while \(mL\) and \(\epsilon_T(x_i)\) test whether the far end can be ignored; they are separate conclusions obtained from the same fit.

Relative Thermistor Cross-Calibration¶

The rod experiment measures temperature differences and spatial temperature gradients. It does not require an absolute temperature calibration. It does require all thermistor channels to give the same reading when they experience the same temperature; otherwise, channel-to-channel offsets could be mistaken for a temperature gradient along the rod.

Use the TEC thermistor as the reference channel and cross-calibrate the rod thermistors as follows:

Step 1. Arrange the sensors. Complete this cross-calibration before the thermistors are permanently installed in the TEC base and rod. All six sensing ends must remain accessible; do not immerse the assembled TEC, heater, or electrical hardware. If you don't have enough prepared thermistors, solder wires to the thermistors and insulate each joint with heat shrink. The hardware page shows how the finished sensor should look.

Label the thermistor intended for the TEC base TEC and label the others 1-5. The labels must remain attached for the rest of the semester. Place all six thermistor sensing ends close together in a Styrofoam cup of warm water over the approximate range \(20^{\circ}\mathrm{C}<T<60^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). Immerse only waterproof sensing ends and keep electrical joints dry. Do not allow the sensors to touch the cup walls or bottom.

Step 2. Use the experimental measurement chain. Make sure the corresponding Arduino circuit and thermistor are always used together. Use the same voltage dividers, Arduino inputs, acquisition code, and sample rate that will be used for the rod experiment.

Step 3. Keep the bath uniform. Stir the water gently and continuously so that all sensors experience the same temperature.

Step 4. Record the common cooling history. Record every channel simultaneously while the water cools slowly through the temperature range expected in the rod experiment.

Step 5. Fit each channel to the reference. Plot each rod-sensor reading, \(T_i\), against the simultaneous TEC-thermistor reading, \(T_{\mathrm{TEC}}\). Do the data fall on a straight line? If so, fit

\[ T_{\mathrm{TEC}}=a_i+b_iT_i. \]

Apply this fitted relation as the correction:

\[ T_{i,\mathrm{corrected}}=a_i+b_iT_i. \]

Step 6. Check the corrected differences. Plot

\[ \Delta T_i =T_{i,\mathrm{corrected}}-T_{\mathrm{TEC}} \]

against \(T_{\mathrm{TEC}}\). Report the mean difference, scatter, and any remaining trend with temperature. Choose and record an acceptance criterion before evaluating the correction. A useful starting criterion is a corrected mean difference smaller than \(0.2^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) for every channel, with no residual trend larger than \(0.2^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) across the calibration range. Revise this criterion if the measured noise and resolution justify a different value.

Step 7. Preserve the raw readings. Store the correction coefficients separately and apply them during analysis; never overwrite the original data.

No independent reference thermometer is required for this relative cross-calibration. The corrected channels may share a common absolute bias, but that bias does not affect temperature differences or the measured spatial profile.

Part 2: Choose Sensor Positions, Then Measure The Step Response¶

  1. Install rod thermistor 1 in the first rod hole and use that hole as the analysis base \(x=0\). It is neither the physical rod end nor the separate thermistor embedded in the TEC. Its measured signal \(T(0,t)\) is the boundary condition for the rod model.
  2. For a steady placement pilot, begin with the TEC set to \(45^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). A lower temperature generally reaches steady state faster but may produce temperature differences that are too small to resolve. A higher temperature improves the spatial signal but increases equilibration time and heat loss. If you change the pilot temperature, use the lowest setpoint that gives a clear profile and never exceed \(55^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).

  3. Move the other four thermistors among the available fixed holes and measure the resulting steady temperatures. No advance calculation of the positions is required. Choose locations for which the steady excess temperature drops by at least a factor of \(e\) from rod thermistor 1 to the farthest retained sensor:

    \[ 0<\theta_5(x_5,\infty) \leq\frac{\theta_1(0,\infty)}{e}. \]

    Plot the pilot values as \(\ln\theta\) versus \(x\) while selecting the holes. The retained points should be spread across the semilog plot rather than clustered near one end. Do not retain a position whose excess temperature is indistinguishable from noise or room drift.

  4. Once you have identified useful locations, leave the thermistors in those holes. Measure and record every selected position and its uncertainty.

  5. Set the TEC to \(20^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and allow the entire rod to equilibrate. Record room temperature and all six thermistor channels. Verify that the rod channels agree within the calibration criterion.
  6. Apply a non-oscillatory TEC step from \(20^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) to \(45^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) using PI control. Record continuously until the stationary-profile criterion below is satisfied.
  7. Plot all temperatures against the same time axis and explain why farther sensors respond later and with smaller excursions.
  8. Save the commanded TEC temperature, measured TEC temperature, and measured rod temperature \(T(0,t)\). The rod thermistor at \(x=0\), rather than the TEC command or TEC thermistor, supplies the model boundary condition.

Part 3: Stationary-Fin Profile¶

  1. Before the run, record the predicted profile and select a numerical steady-state criterion. A useful starting criterion is \(|dT_i/dt|<0.02^{\circ}\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{min}\) at every sensor for at least 10 minutes; justify any different criterion.
