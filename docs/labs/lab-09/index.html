Module 8, Part I: The Angstrom Method¶

Module 8 has two linked parts. This page develops and applies the Angstrom method. Continue with Part II: Conductivity, Heat Loss, And Final Synthesis.

Purpose¶

In this module the TEC controller creates a sinusoidal temperature boundary at the base of the aluminum rod. Thermal diffusion causes the oscillation amplitude to decrease and its phase to lag with distance. Measuring both effects allows you to separate thermal diffusion from heat loss to the air.

Module 8 has three priorities, in this order:

  1. Implement Angstrom method This is the primary objective. Set the TEC temperature using PI control to \(T_{\mathrm{set}}(t)=T_a+T_b\sin(\omega t)\). Produce a trustworthy, synchronized record from the TEC thermistor and rod thermistors T1-T5, display all six traces live, and preserve the same data with the serial logger.
  2. Understand the Angstrom method. Connect sinusoidal thermal diffusion to amplitude decay and phase lag, and use those relationships to infer thermal parameters.
  3. Compare experiment and theory. Use fitted traces, spatial trends, residuals, repeated frequencies, and the Module 7 validity checks to decide how well the heat-transport model describes the rod.

The sinusoidal boundary, autonomous schedule, and logger handoff are means of obtaining the six-temperature record required for these three objectives.

Learning Objectives¶

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  1. Measure all six temperature histories: display the TEC and T1-T5 live, record the same serial stream on the logger, recover and verify the stored file, and preserve sensor positions, units, timestamps, and run metadata.
  2. Understand and apply the Angstrom method: create and verify a sinusoidal rod-base boundary, explain why amplitude decreases and phase lag increases with position, and fit the data to obtain \(q\), \(q'\), \(\kappa\), and \(\nu\) with units and uncertainty.
  3. Compare experiment and theory: use per-sensor fits, spatial trends, residuals, frequency consistency, and the finite-length and transverse-Biot checks from Module 7 to judge the one-dimensional model.
  4. Demonstrate autonomous operation: execute a persistent frequency schedule and make a controlled transition from the live GUI to the Arduino and logger without losing the documented experimental protocol.

Reuse The Validated Sensor Map¶

Module 7 established the geometry and validated six thermistor measurement chains: one thermistor in the TEC base and five thermistors at measured positions along the 6061-T6 aluminum rod. Keep the same coordinate origin at the first rod hole, channel assignments, common-bath result, any optional correction coefficients, measured diameter, analysis length \(L\) from the first hole to the tip, and sensor positions for the Angstrom experiment. Do not move a rod sensor without updating the sensor map and its position uncertainty.

The thermistor embedded in the TEC measures the driven actuator, but it is not the rod boundary at \(x=0\) and is not one of the five rod coordinates. Rod thermistor 1 in the first hole measures \(T(0,t)\), the boundary condition used in the Angstrom model. You will use the Angstrom measurement to determine thermal conductivity rather than substitute a supplied final value. Literature values of \(k\) may guide planning and model-validity checks.

This choice of origin does not change the Angstrom result. Shifting \(x=0\) changes the reference amplitude and phase, but the fitted spatial decay and phase coefficients depend on amplitude ratios, phase differences, and sensor separations. Use the first hole as \(x=0\) consistently in every plot and fit.

In principle, the Angstrom method needs temperatures at only two distinct rod positions. The experiment could therefore be repeated for several pairs of positions. Five simultaneous rod measurements are preferable because they provide multiple amplitude and phase differences in one run, support spatial fits and residual checks, and avoid comparing pairs acquired under different room or boundary conditions.

Before the pilot run, verify that the saved diagram or photograph identifies the TEC-base channel, the five rod channels, the positive \(x\) direction, all five measured rod positions, the analysis length from the first hole to the tip, the overall physical length, and the measured diameter.

Periodic Solution¶

Historical drawing of Angstrom's method for determining thermal conductivity. The course replaces the alternating hot and cold reservoirs with a TEC-controlled sinusoidal boundary temperature.

Take a few minutes to look at the 1863 English translation of Angstrom's original work. You are not expected to follow every line. Focus on the apparatus, the recorded observations, and the way the measurements are connected to the theory. Recognize the kinship between Angstrom and ourselves; the scientific process isn't different 165 years later, although the tools have progressed.

Start With The Rod Equation¶

Use excess temperature \(\theta=T-T_\infty\) and the rod equation

\[ \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} =\kappa\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2}-\nu\theta. \]

Here \(\kappa\) is thermal diffusivity in \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(\nu\) is the lateral heat-loss rate coefficient in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Every term has units of K/s. We assume constant material properties, linear heat loss, constant environmental temperature, and a temperature uniform across each cross section. Module 7 denoted thermal diffusivity by \(\alpha\); here \(\kappa\equiv\alpha=k/(\rho c)\). The symbol \(T_\infty\) does not mean the temperature at the other end of the universe. It is the temperature of the surrounding room, which we treat as a large thermodynamic reservoir whose temperature is not changed appreciably by the rod. We initially treat the rod as semi-infinite, with \(x\geq0\).

Use the measured temperature T1 at the analysis base as

\[ \theta(0,t)=\theta_b\cos(\omega t), \qquad \omega=\frac{2\pi}{\tau}, \]

where \(\tau\) is the period in seconds and \(\omega\) is angular frequency in rad/s. The TEC and its embedded thermistor create and monitor the forcing, but the measured rod temperature at the first hole supplies this boundary condition. We seek the settled periodic response after initial transients decay. If the base oscillates about a mean above room temperature, the full solution also contains a stationary mean profile. By linearity, we can solve for that profile and the oscillating component separately; the derivation below gives the oscillating component.

Before continuing, make a qualitative prediction. Sketch temperature versus time at the base and at two increasing distances along the rod. Predict which features remain the same, which change with distance, and what a shorter drive period should do to the attenuation and phase lag. Return to this sketch after the derivation and pilot run.

Preview The Six-Sensor Angstrom Experiment¶

Open the interactive six-sensor Angstrom simulator. This version extends the finite-rod simulator used in Module 7. Select the Angstrom sinusoidal drive and begin with

\[ T_{\mathrm{set}}(t)=T_a+T_b\sin(\omega t), \qquad T_a=40^\circ\mathrm{C},\quad T_b=16^\circ\mathrm{C},\quad \tau=200\ \mathrm{s}. \]

The simulator displays the driven temperature and the live responses at six positions along the finite rod. Compare their amplitudes and phase delays, and then change the forcing period to predict which sensor positions will provide measurable signals in the physical experiment. The simulator labels its six modeled rod positions T0-T5; in the apparatus, the embedded TEC sensor is separate and the five rod sensors are named T1-T5.

Suggested simulation-only exploration. The initial run now uses these settings. Let the response settle into a repeatable periodic state:

\[ T_a=40^\circ\mathrm{C},\qquad T_b=16^\circ\mathrm{C},\qquad \tau=200\ \mathrm{s}, \]

This simulated setpoint ranges from \(24^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(56^\circ\mathrm{C}\); it is an exploration setting, not permission to exceed the \(55^\circ\mathrm{C}\) hardware limit.

For each modeled position, estimate the time-averaged temperature \(\overline{T}(x)\), oscillation amplitude \(B(x)\), and phase lag relative to T0. Look for four features:

  1. The mean excess temperature, \(\overline{T}(x)-T_\infty\), falls approximately exponentially with position.
  2. The oscillation amplitude \(B(x)\) also falls approximately exponentially, but generally with a different decay coefficient.
  3. The phase lag increases monotonically downstream: each successive temperature trace reaches corresponding points in its cycle later.
  4. Compare the PWM phase with both the requested setpoint and the temperature traces. The PWM should generally lead the measured thermal response because actuator effort precedes the temperature change it produces. Its phase relative to the requested setpoint is not universal: it depends on the controller type and gains, the TEC and rod dynamics, and any saturation. A P-only command is in phase with the instantaneous error; adding the integral term changes the command phase. Measure these phase relationships rather than assuming that PWM always leads the setpoint.

Test the two exponential statements by plotting \(\ln[\overline{T}(x)-T_\infty]\) and \(\ln B(x)\) against \(x\). Compare their slopes, and inspect whether points near the finite tip depart systematically from straight lines.

What model is being simulated?

This simulator uses the same finite-difference rod and dynamic TEC model as the Module 7 simulator. Its rod elements obey

\[ \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} =\kappa\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2}-\nu\theta, \qquad \theta=T-T_\infty, \]

with a finite, zero-flux far tip. The TEC has separate hot- and cold-side thermal states and includes Peltier transport, Joule heating, internal conduction, rod contact, and heat-sink loss. A PID controller drives bounded heating and cooling PWM so that the T0 sensor follows the requested sinusoidal setpoint. Each of the six displayed thermistors has the same first-order sensor lag.

Unlike the semi-infinite analytic solution derived below, the numerical model retains the finite tip and actuator dynamics. It therefore lets you explore where the exponential amplitude and phase relations work well and where the ideal Angstrom assumptions begin to fail.

Full complex-exponential derivation

Guess A Complex Exponential¶

A complex exponential is useful because differentiation returns a constant times the same function. Guess

\[ \widetilde{\theta}(x,t)=\theta_b e^{-sx}e^{i\omega t}. \]

The physical temperature is the real part of this expression. Because the equation is linear with real coefficients, the real part of a complex solution also satisfies the equation. Allowing \(s\) to be complex lets us describe both amplitude decay and phase delay along the rod. At \(x=0\), taking the real part gives the specified cosine boundary condition.

The derivatives are

\[ \frac{\partial\widetilde{\theta}}{\partial t} =i\omega\widetilde{\theta}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2\widetilde{\theta}}{\partial x^2} =s^2\widetilde{\theta}. \]

Substitution into the rod equation and cancellation of the common exponential turn the differential equation into algebra:

\[ i\omega=\kappa s^2-\nu, \qquad \boxed{s^2=\frac{\nu+i\omega}{\kappa}}. \]

The two spatial solutions are \(e^{-sx}\) and \(e^{+sx}\). Choose the square root with positive real part and discard the growing solution \(e^{+sx}\), because the temperature oscillation must vanish far from the driven end.

Separate Amplitude Decay From Phase Delay¶

Write \(s=q+iq'\), with \(q>0\) and \(q'>0\) for positive driving frequency. The prime in \(q'\) is part of its name, not a derivative. Squaring gives

\[ s^2=q^2-q'^2+2iqq'. \]

Equating real and imaginary parts with \((\nu+i\omega)/\kappa\) gives

\[ \boxed{q^2-q'^2=\frac{\nu}{\kappa}}, \qquad \boxed{2qq'=\frac{\omega}{\kappa}}. \]

To find the two coefficients explicitly, use

\[ (q^2+q'^2)^2=(q^2-q'^2)^2+(2qq')^2 =\frac{\nu^2+\omega^2}{\kappa^2}. \]

Thus \(q^2+q'^2=\sqrt{\nu^2+\omega^2}/\kappa\). Adding and subtracting the expressions for this sum and for \(q^2-q'^2\) gives the amplitude-decay and phase coefficients, both in inverse meters:

\[ q=\sqrt{\frac{\nu+\sqrt{\nu^2+\omega^2}}{2\kappa}}, \qquad q'=\sqrt{\frac{-\nu+\sqrt{\nu^2+\omega^2}}{2\kappa}}. \]

The frequency of the drive therefore sets two useful spatial scales. The oscillatory decay length \(1/q\) is the distance over which the oscillation amplitude falls by a factor of \(e\). The spatial wavelength \(2\pi/q'\) is the distance over which the oscillation accumulates a full \(2\pi\) phase lag. For comparison, the stationary temperature profile has decay length \(1/q_1=\sqrt{\kappa/\nu}\).

Characteristic lengths calculated for a half-inch-diameter aluminum rod. Longer periods penetrate farther into the rod but produce less phase change per unit length. Shorter periods produce more phase change per unit length but also attenuate more rapidly. The experimental period should give both a measurable amplitude and a resolvable phase difference across several sensors.

Take The Real Part¶

The complex solution can now be written

\[ \widetilde{\theta}(x,t)=\theta_b e^{-qx}e^{i(\omega t-q'x)}. \]

Taking its real part gives

\[ \boxed{\theta(x,t)=\theta_b e^{-qx}\cos(\omega t-q'x)}. \]

At every position, temperature oscillates at the imposed frequency. Its amplitude decreases as \(e^{-qx}\) and it lags the base by \(q'x\) radians, corresponding to a time delay \(q'x/\omega\). This wave-like pattern is a diffusive response, not ordinary wave propagation.

Allowing an arbitrary base amplitude \(B_0\) and phase \(\phi_0\) gives the form used to fit the experiment:

\[ \theta(x,t)=B_0e^{-qx}\cos(\omega t-q'x+\phi_0). \]

For the boundary condition chosen above, \(B_0=\theta_b\) and \(\phi_0=0\).

Recover Diffusivity And Heat Loss From Measurements¶

For two sensors separated by \(\Delta x=x_2-x_1>0\), let their oscillation amplitudes be \(B_1>B_2\), and let the downstream sensor lag by \(\Delta\phi=\phi_1-\phi_2>0\) radians after phase unwrapping. Then

\[ q=\frac{\ln(B_1/B_2)}{\Delta x}, \qquad q'=\frac{\Delta\phi}{\Delta x}. \]

With several sensors, fit the slopes of log amplitude and unwrapped phase versus position. Rearranging the real- and imaginary-part equations gives the useful inverse relations:

\[ \kappa=\frac{\omega}{2qq'}, \qquad \nu=\kappa(q^2-q'^2). \]
Numerical sanity check

For example, suppose a 100-second run gives \(\omega=0.0628\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), \(q=25\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\), and \(q'=18\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\). Then

\[ \kappa=\frac{0.0628}{2(25)(18)} =6.98\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}, \]

and

\[ \nu=\kappa(25^2-18^2) =2.10\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}. \]

Use this only as a unit and order-of-magnitude check; it is not a supplied result for the course rod.

Amplitude attenuation and phase delay provide two independent measurements, allowing us to separate thermal diffusivity from lateral heat loss. Thermal conductivity follows from \(k=\rho c\kappa\), where \(\rho\) is density in \(\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(c\) is specific heat capacity in \(\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\); thus \(k\) has units \(\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\).

As a check, with no lateral loss, \(\nu=0\), so

\[ q=q'=\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{2\kappa}}. \]

Positive lateral heat loss makes \(q>q'\). Therefore a fitted result with \(q\leq q'\), a negative \(\nu\), or strongly frequency-dependent \(\kappa\) is evidence to investigate the data, uncertainty, settling criterion, or model assumptions rather than a number to repair silently. A shorter driving period produces stronger attenuation and phase change per unit distance. These expressions describe the settled periodic response; they do not include startup transients. A finite rod generally requires both spatial exponentials to satisfy its far-end boundary condition.

These equations use a one-dimensional, semi-infinite rod. Before selecting sensors for the fit, carry forward the finite-length error and transverse-Biot checks from Module 7. State which sensors and conditions satisfy your chosen approximation tolerances, and then use the measured residuals and frequency-consistency test to determine whether those approximations were adequate in practice.

Stage 1: Establish The Boundary Condition¶

Drive the TEC with a sinusoidal setpoint

\[ \boxed{T_{\mathrm{set}}(t)=T_a+T_b\sin(\omega t)}, \qquad \omega=\frac{2\pi}{\tau}. \]

Here \(T_a\) is the mean TEC setpoint, \(T_b\) is its oscillation amplitude, \(\tau\) is the forcing period, and \(\omega\) is the angular frequency. For the first classroom run, use the suggested values

\[ T_a=40^\circ\mathrm{C}, \qquad T_b=8^\circ\mathrm{C}, \qquad \tau=100\ \mathrm{s}, \qquad \omega=\frac{2\pi}{100\ \mathrm{s}} \approx 0.0628\ \mathrm{rad/s}. \]

The resulting requested TEC profile is

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathrm{set}}(t) =40^\circ\mathrm{C} +8^\circ\mathrm{C}\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{100\ \mathrm{s}}\right) }. \]

This has a mean of \(40^\circ\mathrm{C}\), an amplitude of \(8^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and a range from \(32^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(48^\circ\mathrm{C}\). We assume an initial room temperature of approximately \(22^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to simplify the starting protocol, but you must measure and record the actual room temperature for the excess-temperature analysis. The requested TEC temperature is the controller input; the measured temperature T1 at \(x=0\) is the boundary condition used in the Angstrom analysis.

Compensate The Heating-Cooling Asymmetry¶

Generating this sinusoid requires the TEC to alternate between heating and cooling. Your Module 4 measurements showed that equal-magnitude PWM commands do not produce equal temperature responses in the two directions. Let \(\chi_h\) and \(\chi_c\) denote the positive magnitudes of the measured heating and cooling susceptibilities, respectively, and define

\[ \boxed{r=\frac{\chi_h}{\chi_c}}. \]

A useful starting estimate is \(\chi_c\approx\chi_h/2\), so \(r\approx2\): cooling may require approximately twice the PWM magnitude of heating for the same temperature response. Use the ratio measured for your own TEC rather than assuming that it is exactly two.

The feedback response is slow enough that the controller should not rely on the integral term alone to discover this asymmetry. First calculate the ordinary signed PI command

\[ u_{\mathrm{PI}}=K_p e+K_i\int e\,dt, \qquad e=T_{\mathrm{set}}-T_{\mathrm{TEC}}. \]

Then apply the measured heating-cooling ratio before limiting the command:

\[ u_{\mathrm{cmd}}= \begin{cases} u_{\mathrm{PI}}, & u_{\mathrm{PI}}\geq0 \quad\text{(heating)},\\[4pt] r\,u_{\mathrm{PI}}, & u_{\mathrm{PI}}<0 \quad\text{(cooling)}. \end{cases} \]

The sign of \(u_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) selects the H-bridge direction, and its magnitude sets the PWM after clamping it to the safe range. This measured, direction-dependent scaling is a static actuator compensation, also called static feedforward compensation. It linearizes the unequal heating and cooling gains by supplying the known extra cooling command immediately; it does not predict the rod's dynamic response. PI feedback corrects the remaining tracking error. Apply saturation and anti-windup to the compensated command, and never energize both H-bridge direction inputs simultaneously.

  1. Measure and record room temperature before starting the run.
  2. Start with the 100-second minimum experiment specified in the two-tier checklist. This is the period used in the course-data figure in Stage 6, so you can compare your first observations with a known example. Its short period lets you observe many cycles, check phase lag, and debug the acquisition system promptly.
  3. Enter the measured values of \(\chi_h\) and \(\chi_c\), calculate \(r\), and verify that a negative controller command receives the cooling multiplier before PWM saturation.
  4. Tune PI settings so the TEC drive produces a rod-base temperature T1 at the first hole that is close to sinusoidal without strong clipping, drift, or phase jumps.
  5. Record the TEC setpoint, measured TEC temperature, raw PI command, compensated signed PWM, direction, and all rod temperatures, T1-T5.
  6. Quantify the TEC tracking and the sinusoidal quality of T1 with amplitude, phase, and residual measures. Report the measured-to-requested amplitude ratio and the phase lag of T1 relative to the requested setpoint.
  7. Use the measured temperature T1, rather than the requested TEC setpoint or TEC-thermistor temperature, as the model boundary condition.
  8. Plot raw \(u_{\mathrm{PI}}\) against applied signed PWM. Identify the two actuator slopes, saturation, and any region in which the compensation does not linearize the heating and cooling response adequately.

Optimize The Waveform From The Pilot Data¶

The best waveform is the one that produces both measurable attenuation and a resolvable phase lag across several rod sensors while remaining safe and close to sinusoidal. Use the live display and pilot fits to adjust it:

  • Aim for the farthest retained sensor to have an oscillation amplitude of at least about 10-20% of the amplitude at \(x=0\). A smaller response may be difficult to distinguish from sensor noise.
  • Aim for an unwrapped phase lag from the first to the farthest retained sensor of roughly 0.5-2 radians. This is a planning target, not a fit constraint.
  • If distant oscillations are too small, first try a longer period. If all sensors have nearly the same phase, try a shorter period.
  • If the measured waveform clips, flattens, or tracks poorly, reduce \(T_b\), reduce the mean temperature, or retune the controller before collecting production data.
  • If the oscillation is clean but too small at every sensor, increase \(T_b\) cautiously. Keep the measured rod-base temperature below \(55^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and retain margin for overshoot.
  • Keep \(T_a\) and \(T_b\) fixed while comparing periods unless the pilot data show that a change is necessary. This makes frequency comparisons easier to interpret.

Use the minimum and extended period requirements in the two-tier checklist. Choose additional periods from the pilot evidence rather than from a fixed list alone.

Stage 4: Build And Test The Autonomous Frequency Schedule¶

The Python GUI controls the pilot run, but it will not be present during an autonomous run. Every team must revise the GUI and Arduino code so the GUI can define a sequence of schedule steps. Each step must specify at least its duration, \(T_a\), \(T_b\), and either \(\omega\) or the period. The Arduino, not Python, must advance the schedule and calculate the setpoint during autonomous operation.

The schedule must remain present after Arduino power is removed. Either compile the approved schedule into a separately named autonomous sketch or have the GUI send it once to nonvolatile Arduino storage and then read it back for verification. Values held only in Arduino RAM do not survive the power transition. Do not write the schedule to EEPROM on every sampling cycle.

Keep the sinusoidal phase continuous when the frequency changes so that the requested boundary temperature does not jump merely because a new schedule step begins. At every step transition, print a metadata/event line and include the active step and forcing parameters in subsequent data records. Preserve the transient after each frequency change; the analysis will later identify the settled interval for that frequency.

Before any autonomous run, test an accelerated one-frequency schedule while the GUI and logger are both attached. Demonstrate startup, safe completion, correct metadata, and logger retrieval. If you plan an extended run, also demonstrate at least two automatic frequency changes before restoring the intended durations and verifying the final schedule.

For a minimum successful experiment, the autonomous schedule may hold one well-resolved frequency long enough to satisfy the settled-data criterion. An extended schedule may sample periods such as 100, 180, 240, 300, and 480 seconds while initially retaining the same \(T_a\) and \(T_b\). Longer periods penetrate farther along the rod but produce less phase change per unit length. Use the pilot data and stated settling criterion to choose step durations; do not assume that every frequency settles on the same schedule.

Stage 5: Rehearse The Live-To-Autonomous Handoff¶

The classroom run and autonomous run are separate records. Every team must demonstrate this handoff using at least a one-frequency schedule. A short power gap will perturb the rod, so the autonomous file must retain the new startup transient and the analysis must exclude it until the response satisfies the stated settling criterion. Minimize the gap, but do not bypass a safe shutdown.

  1. While still connected to the laptop, load or store the final autonomous schedule, read it back, and confirm the logger is receiving valid records.
  2. Record the wall-clock handoff time, schedule version, code commit, intended logger filename, and all power-supply and safety settings.
  3. Command zero TEC output and turn off the separate TEC supply.
  4. Close the GUI, close the logger file using its SAVE input if connected, and wait several seconds for the final write.
  5. Remove Arduino power, disconnect the Arduino USB cable from the laptop, and connect it promptly to the approved USB wall supply. Do not connect two USB power sources to the Uno.
  6. Restore the separate current-limited TEC supply. On reboot, the Arduino must wait for the logger to initialize, print a new run header, load the verified schedule, and begin at schedule step 1 without Python.
  7. Confirm the expected Arduino and logger indicators and complete the instructor's unattended-run safety check before leaving the apparatus.

Rehearse this entire sequence in class. Measure and report the power-off gap. The acceptance criterion is not zero interruption; it is a short, documented, repeatable transition followed by a recognizable autonomous run whose startup transient can be excluded honestly.

Stage 6: Pilot Analysis And Experiment Plan¶

For each sensor, fit

\[ T_i(t)=a_i+B_i\cos(\omega t+\phi_i). \]

With this convention, a downstream phase lag appears as a smaller unwrapped value of \(\phi_i\). Therefore a plot of unwrapped phase versus position has slope \(-q'\). Reversing the sinusoid convention reverses the phase signs but must not change the inferred physics.

The experimental example below shows temperatures measured at two rod positions. The traces have the same temporal frequency, while the downstream response has a smaller amplitude and reaches each peak later.

Course data acquired with a 100-second forcing period, showing the two observables used by the Angstrom method: amplitude decay and phase lag. Fit each trace independently to obtain \(B_i\) and \(\phi_i\); spatial fits of \(\ln B_i\) and unwrapped \(\phi_i\) then determine \(q\) and \(q'\).

  1. Remove the initial transient or justify the selected fitting interval. State what evidence you used to decide that the response was settled.
  2. Fit at least five complete settled periods.
  3. Plot the data and fitted sinusoid for every sensor.
  4. Plot amplitude B_i versus position on a logarithmic vertical scale.
  5. Unwrap phase and plot phase versus position.
  6. Confirm one production period that satisfies the minimum-data gate. If the pilot evidence supports an extended experiment, select additional periods for which several sensors retain measurable amplitude and phase differences.

Your experiment plan must state the period, mean, amplitude, run duration, sampling interval, safety limits, settling criterion, channels, files, and the tests you will use to decide whether the run is usable.

Stage 8: Spatial Fits And Modeling-App Draft¶

Fit

\[ B(x)=B_0e^{-qx}, \qquad \phi(x)=\phi_0-q'x. \]

Your Python app, notebook, or script must visibly show:

  • imported raw data and selected fitting interval,
  • TEC tracking and the measured rod-base waveform,
  • sinusoid fit for every retained sensor,
  • amplitude and unwrapped phase versus position,
  • fitted q and q_prime with units and uncertainty,
  • calculated kappa and nu,
  • residuals and any excluded data with justification,
  • the finite-length and transverse-Biot-number checks that justify the one-dimensional semi-infinite analysis for the retained sensors.

Use the two-stage analysis above as the primary method: first fit each temperature trace to obtain \(B_i\) and \(\phi_i\), and then fit their spatial dependence. This makes individual sensor behavior, phase unwrapping, and exclusions visible. As an optional model-validity extension, fit all retained \(T_i(t)\) data globally with one shared set of spatial parameters and compare the resulting \(q\), \(q'\), residuals, and uncertainties with the transparent two-stage result.
