Module 8, Part I: The Angstrom Method¶

Module 8 has two linked parts. This page develops and applies the Angstrom method. Continue with Part II: Conductivity, Heat Loss, And Final Synthesis.

Purpose¶

The TEC controller creates a sinusoidal temperature boundary at the base of the aluminum rod. Thermal diffusion causes the oscillation amplitude to decrease and its phase to lag with distance. Measuring both effects allows you to separate axial thermal diffusion from heat loss to the air.

The module follows three priorities: obtain a trustworthy six-temperature record, understand the Angstrom method, and compare experiment with theory. The learning objectives state what you must be able to do. The S18 experimental overview previews the measurements and control comparisons so that you can see what the theory must explain. The detailed experimental instructions follow the theory in Stages 1-6.

Learning Objectives¶

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  2. Measure all six temperature histories: display and record the TEC and T1-T5 with the live GUI;

  3. Derive and understand the settled-periodic temperature profile: derive the complete Angstrom solution for a finite rod with an insulated tip by separating the stationary mean and oscillatory parts, applying the boundary conditions, and taking the real part of the complex solution. Interpret the solution physically in terms of three spatial length scales: the mean-temperature decay length \(\ell_{\mathrm{mean}}=1/m\), the oscillation-amplitude decay length \(\ell_{\mathrm{amp}}=1/q\), and the one-radian phase-shift length \(\ell_{\mathrm{phase}}=1/q'\). Relate these scales to one another and to the dimensional parameters \(\kappa\), \(\nu\), and \(\omega\) through \(m^2=\nu/\kappa\), \(m^2=q^2-q'^2\), and \(2qq'=\omega/\kappa\); express their ratios using the dimensionless frequency \(\Omega=\omega/\nu\); and fit the measurements to obtain \(q\), \(q'\), \(\kappa\), and \(\nu\) with units and uncertainty.

  4. Compare experiment and theory: use per-sensor fits, spatial trends, residuals, frequency consistency, and the finite-length and transverse-Biot checks from Module 7 to judge the one-dimensional model.

S18 Experimental Overview¶

A sinusoidal PWM command does not guarantee a sinusoidal temperature. The raw-PWM, feedforward, P, and PI comparisons reveal what the actuator and controller contribute to the measured boundary waveform. Along the rod, the six-temperature record reveals a second question: why does the oscillation shrink and lag with distance? The Angstrom theory connects these observations to thermal diffusion and heat loss, and gives predictions to test at more than one forcing period.

Keep three temperatures distinct: \(T_{\mathrm{set}}\) is requested; \(T_{\mathrm{TEC}}\) measures the actuator; and T1 at the first rod hole is the boundary \(T(0,t)\) used in the rod model. The detailed actions begin in Stage 1.

Choose Sensor Positions For The Quantity Being Measured¶

Module 7 established the geometry and validated six thermistor measurement chains: one thermistor in the TEC base and five thermistors at measured positions along the 6061-T6 aluminum rod. Keep the same coordinate origin at the first rod hole, channel assignments, common-bath result, any optional correction coefficients, measured diameter, and analysis length \(L\) from the first hole to the tip. A sensor may be moved to a different hole, but each measurement configuration must have its own recorded sensor map and position uncertainties.

The thermistor embedded in the TEC measures the driven actuator, but it is not the rod boundary at \(x=0\) and is not one of the five rod coordinates. Rod thermistor 1 in the first hole measures \(T(0,t)\), the boundary condition used in the Angstrom model. You will use the Angstrom measurement to determine thermal conductivity rather than substitute a supplied final value. Literature values of \(k\) may guide planning and model-validity checks.

This choice of origin does not change the Angstrom result. Shifting \(x=0\) changes the reference amplitude and phase, but the fitted spatial decay and phase coefficients depend on amplitude ratios, phase differences, and sensor separations. Use the first hole as \(x=0\) consistently in every plot and fit.

The mean and oscillatory parts require different sensor-placement strategies. For the course rod, the estimated mean-profile length is relatively long: \(mL\approx1.3\). The stationary mean excess temperature therefore remains measurable over the full rod. Module 7 already measured this profile using a full-length sensor configuration and compared it with the finite- and infinite-rod models. Do not repeat that measurement in Module 8. Retain the Module 7 sensor map, data, and fitted mean-decay coefficient \(m\) for comparison with the oscillatory results.

The oscillatory part decays much faster. Over periods from \(100\) to \(500\) s, the estimate is \(qL\approx6.5\) to \(3.0\). In the semi-infinite approximation, the oscillation amplitude at the far end would therefore be only \(e^{-qL}\approx0.0015\) to \(0.05\) of its value at the base. A full-length sensor layout would place too many thermistors where the oscillatory signal is very small.

For the Module 8 Angstrom measurement, begin with T1 in the first hole and define that position as \(x=0\). Place T2-T5 at approximately 1-inch intervals in order of increasing \(x\). This spacing has produced measurable oscillatory signals in prior trials while still providing enough separation to resolve amplitude decay and phase lag. Measure and record every position, and save this oscillatory-measurement map separately from the full-length mean-profile map retained from Module 7.

Treat the 1-inch spacing as a starting point, not a permanent requirement. Collect pilot data at a short and a long usable period before finalizing the layout. If the downstream oscillations are too small to fit, move the sensors closer to the base or closer together. If the amplitudes and phases change too little between neighboring sensors, spread the sensors farther apart. After any repositioning, remeasure every \(x\) coordinate and update the oscillatory sensor map before collecting the production data.

In principle, the Angstrom method needs temperatures at only two distinct rod positions. Five simultaneous rod measurements are preferable because they provide multiple amplitude and phase differences in one run, support spatial fits and residual checks, and avoid comparing pairs acquired under different room or boundary conditions. Use the pilot run to confirm that at least three rod sensors retain measurable amplitude and phase at every selected frequency.

Before the pilot run, verify that the saved diagram or photograph identifies the TEC-base channel, the five rod channels, the positive \(x\) direction, all five measured rod positions, the analysis length from the first hole to the tip, the overall physical length, and the measured diameter.

Periodic Solution¶

Learning Objective 2 in practice — Derive and understand the settled-periodic temperature profile. As you work through this section, follow the complete finite-rod derivation and connect each equation to the three observable spatial scales: mean-temperature decay, oscillation-amplitude decay, and phase shift.

Historical drawing of Angstrom's method for determining thermal conductivity. The course replaces the alternating hot and cold reservoirs with a TEC-controlled sinusoidal boundary temperature.

Take a few minutes to look at the 1863 English translation of Angstrom's original work. You are not expected to follow every line. Start With The Rod Equation¶

Module 7, Part III introduced this rod model and explained each physical term, including its units and the definitions of the transport coefficients.

Use excess temperature \(\theta=T-T_\infty\) and the rod equation

\[ \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} =\kappa\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2}-\nu\theta. \]

Here \(\kappa\) is thermal diffusivity in \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(\nu\) is the lateral heat-loss rate coefficient in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Every term has units of K/s. We assume constant material properties, linear heat loss, constant environmental temperature, and a temperature uniform across each cross section. Module 7 denoted thermal diffusivity by \(\alpha\); here \(\kappa\equiv\alpha=k/(\rho c)\). The symbol \(T_\infty\) does not mean the temperature at the other end of the universe. It is the temperature of the surrounding room, which we treat as a large thermodynamic reservoir whose temperature is not changed appreciably by the rod. We initially treat the rod as semi-infinite, with \(x\geq0\).

Use the measured temperature T1 at the analysis base as

\[ \theta(0,t)=\theta_b\cos(\omega t), \qquad \omega=\frac{2\pi}{\tau}, \]

where \(\tau\) is the period in seconds and \(\omega\) is angular frequency in rad/s. The TEC and its embedded thermistor create and monitor the forcing, but the measured rod temperature at the first hole supplies this boundary condition. We seek the settled periodic response after initial transients decay. If the base oscillates about a mean above room temperature, the full solution also contains a stationary mean profile. By linearity, we can solve for that profile and the oscillating component separately; the derivation below gives the oscillating component.

Before continuing, make a qualitative prediction. Sketch temperature versus time at the base and at two increasing distances along the rod. Predict which features remain the same, which change with distance, and what a shorter drive period should do to the attenuation and phase lag. Return to this sketch after the derivation and pilot run.

What model is being simulated?

This simulator uses the same finite-difference rod and dynamic TEC model as the Module 7 simulator. Its rod elements obey

\[ \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} =\kappa\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x^2}-\nu\theta, \qquad \theta=T-T_\infty, \]

with a finite, zero-flux far tip. The TEC has separate hot- and cold-side thermal states and includes Peltier transport, Joule heating, internal conduction, rod contact, and heat-sink loss. A PID controller drives bounded heating and cooling PWM so that the T0 sensor follows the requested sinusoidal setpoint. Each of the six displayed thermistors has the same first-order sensor lag.

Unlike the semi-infinite analytic solution derived below, the numerical model retains the finite tip and actuator dynamics. It therefore lets you explore where the exponential amplitude and phase relations work well and where the ideal Angstrom assumptions begin to fail.

Full Complex-Exponential Derivation¶

Start From The Space-Time Equation¶

For excess temperature \(\theta(x,t)=T(x,t)-T_\infty\), the semi-infinite rod obeys

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial \theta(x,t)}{\partial t} =\kappa\frac{\partial^2\theta(x,t)}{\partial x^2} -\nu\theta(x,t) }, \qquad x\geq 0. \]

The periodic boundary conditions are

\[ \theta(0,t)=\theta_b\cos(\omega t), \qquad \lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\theta(x,t)=0. \]

Guess A Complex Exponential¶

A complex exponential is useful because differentiation returns a constant times the same function. Guess

\[ \widetilde{\theta}(x,t)=\theta_b e^{-sx}e^{i\omega t}. \]

The physical temperature is the real part of this expression. Because the equation is linear with real coefficients, the real part of a complex solution also satisfies the equation. Allowing \(s\) to be complex lets us describe both amplitude decay and phase delay along the rod. At \(x=0\), taking the real part gives the specified cosine boundary condition.

The derivatives are

\[ \frac{\partial\widetilde{\theta}}{\partial t} =i\omega\widetilde{\theta}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2\widetilde{\theta}}{\partial x^2} =s^2\widetilde{\theta}. \]

Substitution into the rod equation and cancellation of the common exponential turn the differential equation into algebra:

\[ i\omega=\kappa s^2-\nu, \qquad \boxed{s^2=\frac{\nu+i\omega}{\kappa}}. \]

The two spatial solutions are \(e^{-sx}\) and \(e^{+sx}\). Choose the square root with positive real part and discard the growing solution \(e^{+sx}\), because the temperature oscillation must vanish far from the driven end.

Separate Amplitude Decay From Phase Delay¶

Write \(s=q+iq'\), with \(q>0\) and \(q'>0\) for positive driving frequency. The prime in \(q'\) is part of its name, not a derivative. Squaring gives

\[ s^2=q^2-q'^2+2iqq'. \]

Equating real and imaginary parts with \((\nu+i\omega)/\kappa\) gives

\[ \boxed{q^2-q'^2=\frac{\nu}{\kappa}}, \qquad \boxed{2qq'=\frac{\omega}{\kappa}}. \]

To find the two coefficients explicitly, use

\[ (q^2+q'^2)^2=(q^2-q'^2)^2+(2qq')^2 =\frac{\nu^2+\omega^2}{\kappa^2}. \]

Thus \(q^2+q'^2=\sqrt{\nu^2+\omega^2}/\kappa\). Adding and subtracting the expressions for this sum and for \(q^2-q'^2\) gives the amplitude-decay and phase coefficients, both in inverse meters:

\[ q=\sqrt{\frac{\nu+\sqrt{\nu^2+\omega^2}}{2\kappa}}, \qquad q'=\sqrt{\frac{-\nu+\sqrt{\nu^2+\omega^2}}{2\kappa}}. \]

The frequency of the drive therefore sets two useful spatial scales. The oscillatory decay length \(1/q\) is the distance over which the oscillation amplitude falls by a factor of \(e\). The spatial wavelength \(2\pi/q'\) is the distance over which the oscillation accumulates a full \(2\pi\) phase lag. For comparison, the stationary temperature profile has decay length \(1/q_1=\sqrt{\kappa/\nu}\).

Characteristic lengths calculated for a half-inch-diameter aluminum rod. Longer periods penetrate farther into the rod but produce less phase change per unit length. Shorter periods produce more phase change per unit length but also attenuate more rapidly. The experimental period should give both a measurable amplitude and a resolvable phase difference across several sensors.

Take The Real Part¶

The complex solution can now be written

\[ \widetilde{\theta}(x,t)=\theta_b e^{-qx}e^{i(\omega t-q'x)}. \]

Taking its real part gives

\[ \boxed{\theta(x,t)=\theta_b e^{-qx}\cos(\omega t-q'x)}. \]

At every position, temperature oscillates at the imposed frequency. Its amplitude decreases as \(e^{-qx}\) and it lags the base by \(q'x\) radians, corresponding to a time delay \(q'x/\omega\). This wave-like pattern is a diffusive response, not ordinary wave propagation.

Allowing an arbitrary base amplitude \(B_0\) and phase \(\phi_0\) gives the form used to fit the experiment:

\[ \theta(x,t)=B_0e^{-qx}\cos(\omega t-q'x+\phi_0). \]

For the boundary condition chosen above, \(B_0=\theta_b\) and \(\phi_0=0\).

Combine The Mean And Oscillatory Solutions¶

The derivation above gives only the oscillatory part. The imposed boundary temperature also has a time-independent mean:

\[ T(0,t)=T_a+T_b\cos(\omega t). \]

Because the rod equation is linear, add the stationary mean profile to the settled oscillatory response. For a semi-infinite rod, the complete settled-periodic solution is

\[ \boxed{ T(x,t)=T_\infty +(T_a-T_\infty)e^{-mx} +T_b e^{-qx}\cos(\omega t-q'x) }, \]

where

\[ m=\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\kappa}}, \qquad s=q+iq'=\sqrt{\frac{\nu+i\omega}{\kappa}}, \qquad m^2=q^2-q'^2. \]

The first exponential describes the time-averaged temperature above room temperature. The second describes the oscillation about that mean. Thus the mean and the oscillation amplitude both decrease with position, but generally with different decay coefficients, \(m\) and \(q\). This is a settled periodic state, not a time-independent steady state. Using sine instead of cosine only changes the choice of time origin.

The solution contains three physical length scales:

\[ \ell_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{1}{m}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\nu}}, \qquad \ell_{\mathrm{amp}}=\frac{1}{q}, \qquad \ell_{\mathrm{phase}}=\frac{1}{q'}. \]

Over \(\ell_{\mathrm{mean}}\), the mean excess temperature falls by a factor of \(e\). Over \(\ell_{\mathrm{amp}}\), the oscillation amplitude falls by a factor of \(e\). Over \(\ell_{\mathrm{phase}}\), the oscillation accumulates one radian of phase lag; its spatial wavelength is therefore \(2\pi\ell_{\mathrm{phase}}\).

Define the dimensionless driving frequency

\[ \Omega=\frac{\omega}{\nu}. \]

The three scales are not independent. Dividing the relations for \(q\) and \(q'\) by \(m=\sqrt{\nu/\kappa}\) gives

\[ \frac{q}{m} =\sqrt{\frac{1+\sqrt{1+\Omega^2}}{2}}, \qquad \frac{q'}{m} =\sqrt{\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+\Omega^2}}{2}}. \]

Equivalently, \(\ell_{\mathrm{amp}}/\ell_{\mathrm{mean}}=m/q\) and \(\ell_{\mathrm{phase}}/\ell_{\mathrm{mean}}=m/q'\). For a finite rod, the dimensionless lengths \(mL\), \(qL\), and \(q'L\) state how many mean-decay, amplitude-decay, and one-radian phase-shift lengths fit within the rod.

Guided Student Solution: The Finite Insulated-Tip Rod¶

For a finite rod, let the analysis base be at \(x=0\) and the insulated tip be at \(x=L\). The boundary conditions are

\[ T(0,t)=T_a+T_b\cos(\omega t), \qquad \left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L}=0. \]

Step 1: Separate the mean and oscillatory parts. Write

\[ T(x,t)=T_\infty+\overline{\theta}(x) +\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{osc}}(x,t)\right\}. \]

The stationary part obeys

\[ \frac{d^2\overline{\theta}}{dx^2}-m^2\overline{\theta}=0, \qquad \overline{\theta}(0)=T_a-T_\infty, \qquad \overline{\theta}'(L)=0. \]

Use the finite insulated-tip solution from Module 7 to obtain

\[ \boxed{ \overline{\theta}(x) =(T_a-T_\infty) \frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)} }. \]

Step 2: Solve the oscillatory spatial problem. Use

\[ \widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{osc}}(x,t)=F(x)e^{i\omega t}. \]

Substitution into the rod equation gives

\[ F''-s^2F=0, \qquad s^2=\frac{\nu+i\omega}{\kappa}. \]

Apply

\[ F(0)=T_b, \qquad F'(L)=0. \]

The finite-rod oscillatory solution is

\[ \boxed{ \widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{osc}}(x,t) =T_b \frac{\cosh[s(L-x)]}{\cosh(sL)} e^{i\omega t} }. \]

Step 3: Combine the two pieces and take the real part. The complete finite-rod settled-periodic solution is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} T(x,t)=T_\infty &+(T_a-T_\infty) \frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)} \\ &+\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{ T_b\frac{\cosh[s(L-x)]}{\cosh(sL)}e^{i\omega t} \right\}. \end{aligned} } \]

Define

\[ \frac{\cosh[s(L-x)]}{\cosh(sL)} =A(x)e^{-i\phi(x)}. \]

Here \(A(x)\) is the dimensionless oscillation amplitude relative to the base, so the temperature-oscillation amplitude is \(T_bA(x)\), and \(\phi(x)\) is the phase lag. With \(s=q+iq'\), its explicit magnitude is

\[ A(x)=\left|\frac{\cosh[s(L-x)]}{\cosh(sL)}\right| =\sqrt{\frac{\cosh[2q(L-x)]+\cos[2q'(L-x)]} {\cosh(2qL)+\cos(2q'L)}}. \]

In particular, \(A(0)=1\). Then the same result can be written entirely with real quantities:

\[ T(x,t)=T_\infty +(T_a-T_\infty) \frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)} +T_bA(x)\cos[\omega t-\phi(x)]. \]

Expanding the complex hyperbolic functions gives an explicit expression for \(T(x,t)\) without the amplitude and phase functions:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} T(x,t)={}&T_\infty +(T_a-T_\infty)\frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)} \\[6pt] &+T_b\frac{ \cosh[q(2L-x)]\cos(q'x)+\cosh(qx)\cos[q'(2L-x)] }{\cosh(2qL)+\cos(2q'L)}\cos(\omega t) \\[6pt] &+T_b\frac{ \sinh[q(2L-x)]\sin(q'x)+\sinh(qx)\sin[q'(2L-x)] }{\cosh(2qL)+\cos(2q'L)}\sin(\omega t). \end{aligned} } \]

The spatial parameters \(m\), \(q\), and \(q'\) are defined above by \(m^2=\nu/\kappa\), \(q^2-q'^2=\nu/\kappa\), and \(2qq'=\omega/\kappa\), with \(q,q'>0\).

Checks for you to complete:

Check 1. Set \(x=0\) and verify that the solution gives \(T(0,t)=T_a+T_b\cos(\omega t)\).

Check 2. Differentiate with respect to \(x\) and verify that both contributions have zero slope at \(x=L\).

Check 3. Show that when \(mL\gg1\) and \(qL\gg1\),

\[ \frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)}\rightarrow e^{-mx}, \qquad \frac{\cosh[s(L-x)]}{\cosh(sL)}\rightarrow e^{-sx}. \]

Thus the finite solution reduces to the semi-infinite solution above.

Recover Diffusivity And Heat Loss From Measurements¶

For two sensors separated by \(\Delta x=x_2-x_1>0\), let their oscillation amplitudes be \(B_1>B_2\), and let the downstream sensor lag by \(\Delta\phi=\phi_1-\phi_2>0\) radians after phase unwrapping. Then

\[ q=\frac{\ln(B_1/B_2)}{\Delta x}, \qquad q'=\frac{\Delta\phi}{\Delta x}. \]

With several sensors, fit the slopes of log amplitude and unwrapped phase versus position. Rearranging the real- and imaginary-part equations gives the useful inverse relations:

\[ \kappa=\frac{\omega}{2qq'}, \qquad \nu=\kappa(q^2-q'^2). \]

This is an important experimental advantage of the Angstrom method. Thermal conductivity follows directly from the product of the measured amplitude-decay and phase slopes:

\[ \boxed{k=\rho c\kappa =\rho c\frac{\omega}{2qq'}}. \]

Determining \(k\) therefore does not require an independent measurement of the convective coefficient \(h\) or the total heat lost from the rod. Such a measurement would be difficult because convection depends on airflow, orientation, surface condition, and temperature. Instead, the amplitude and phase measurements contain enough independent information to separate axial diffusion from convective side loss.

Numerical sanity check

For example, suppose a 100-second run gives \(\omega=0.0628\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), \(q=25\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\), and \(q'=18\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\). Then

\[ \kappa=\frac{0.0628}{2(25)(18)} =6.98\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}, \]

and

\[ \nu=\kappa(25^2-18^2) =2.10\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}. \]

Use this only as a unit and order-of-magnitude check; it is not a supplied result for the course rod.

The second inverse relation separately gives the side-loss rate \(\nu\), from which the convective coefficient \(h\) can also be inferred. Here \(\rho\) is density in \(\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(c\) is specific heat capacity in \(\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\), so \(k\) has units \(\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\).

As a check, with no lateral loss, \(\nu=0\), so

\[ q=q'=\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{2\kappa}}. \]

Positive lateral heat loss makes \(q>q'\). Therefore a fitted result with \(q\leq q'\), a negative \(\nu\), or strongly frequency-dependent \(\kappa\) is evidence to investigate the data, uncertainty, settling criterion, or model assumptions rather than a number to repair silently. A shorter driving period produces stronger attenuation and phase change per unit distance. These expressions describe the settled periodic response; they do not include startup transients. A finite rod generally requires both spatial exponentials to satisfy its far-end boundary condition.

These equations use a one-dimensional, semi-infinite rod. Before selecting sensors for the fit, carry forward the finite-length error and transverse-Biot checks from Module 7. State which sensors and conditions satisfy your chosen approximation tolerances, and then use the measured residuals and frequency-consistency test to determine whether those approximations were adequate in practice.

S18.5: Specify The Setpoint And Construct Feedforward¶

Specify the requested sinusoidal temperature setpoint

\[ \boxed{T_{\mathrm{set}}(t)=T_a+T_b\sin(\omega t)}, \qquad \omega=\frac{2\pi}{\tau}. \]

\[ T_a=40^\circ\mathrm{C}, \qquad T_b=16^\circ\mathrm{C}, \qquad \tau=100\ \mathrm{s}, \qquad \omega=\frac{2\pi}{100\ \mathrm{s}} \approx 0.0628\ \mathrm{rad/s}. \]

\[ \boxed{ T_{\mathrm{set}}(t) =40^\circ\mathrm{C} +16^\circ\mathrm{C}\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{100\ \mathrm{s}}\right) }. \]

This has a mean of \(40^\circ\mathrm{C}\), an amplitude of \(16^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and a requested range from \(24^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(56^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The \(56^\circ\mathrm{C}\) measured rod-base safety limit still takes precedence; reduce \(T_a\) or \(T_b\) if necessary to prevent tracking error or overshoot from exceeding that limit. We assume an initial room temperature of approximately \(22^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to simplify the starting protocol, but use the actual room temperature measured in S18.3 for the excess-temperature analysis. The requested TEC temperature is the controller input; the measured temperature T1 at \(x=0\) is the boundary condition used in the Angstrom analysis.

Load the measured heating and cooling susceptibility curves. Generating the temperature sinusoid may require PWM of either sign. Your Module 4 measurements showed that equal-magnitude PWM commands do not produce equal temperature responses in the two directions. Let \(\chi_h\) and \(\chi_c\) denote the positive magnitudes of the measured heating and cooling susceptibilities, respectively, and calculate

\[ \boxed{r=\frac{\chi_h}{\chi_c}}. \]

A useful starting estimate is \(\chi_c\approx\chi_h/2\), so \(r\approx2\): cooling may require approximately twice the PWM magnitude of heating for the same temperature response. Use the ratio measured for your own TEC rather than assuming that it is exactly two.

Remember that a sine wave is symmetric about its mean:

\[ T_{\mathrm{set}}\!\left(t+\frac{\tau}{2}\right)-T_a =-\left[T_{\mathrm{set}}(t)-T_a\right]. \]

The desired temperature therefore spends equal time above and below \(T_a\) and has equal positive and negative excursions. The required PWM need not have this symmetry because heating and cooling have different gains and dynamics. In an open-loop trial, this difference may appear as unequal heating and cooling slopes, unequal extrema, an offset from the intended mean, or waveform distortion.

After completing the two raw-PWM trials, do not mistake a sinusoidal PWM for a feedforward temperature command. Use your measured steady-state susceptibility curve to determine the PWM required at each desired temperature. If the calibration is written as

\[ T_{\mathrm{ss}}=f(u), \]

construct an interpolation or lookup table for the inverse relation and apply

\[ u_{\mathrm{ff}}(t)=f^{-1}\!\left(T_{\mathrm{set}}(t)\right). \]

Use the appropriate measured heating or cooling branch when the calibration distinguishes them. This is an open-loop feedforward command: it uses the steady-state \(\chi\) calibration but does not correct the instantaneous temperature error.

Predict, at each point in the cycle, which value of \(u\) should produce that temperature. Plot the predicted \(u_{\mathrm{ff}}(t)\) before applying it and explain why the required PWM waveform need not itself be sinusoidal.

Apply the same measured heating-cooling compensation and safety limits in both feedback modes. For example, the PI command becomes

\[ u_{\mathrm{cmd}}= \begin{cases} u_{\mathrm{PI}}, & u_{\mathrm{PI}}\geq0 \quad\text{(heating)},\\[4pt] r\,u_{\mathrm{PI}}, & u_{\mathrm{PI}}<0 \quad\text{(cooling)}. \end{cases} \]

The sign of \(u_{\mathrm{cmd}}\) selects the H-bridge direction, and its magnitude sets the PWM after clamping it to the safe range.
